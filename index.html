Discounts, allshore, fixed price -- these get called creative constantly. They're not. They're the price of entry now. Real commercial creativity changes who carries the risk, not just the number on the invoice.

What people call creative
A lower rate, a friendlier payment term, a cheaper location.
What's actually creative
A structure where Slalom and the client both win -- or both feel it -- on the same outcome.
Why This Matters

A discount costs more than the number suggests.

The reason to restructure rather than discount isn't philosophical -- it's arithmetic. Here's what actually happens to a healthy-looking engagement margin on its way to the bottom line.

52%
Engagement margin

What most people look at, and traditionally considered healthy. Covers people cost -- office, technology, real estate -- but not much else.

42%
Account margin

After business development and account management costs (around 10% on average), including pipeline support for deals both won and lost.

12%
Operating income

After allocated overhead of roughly 62.5%. On a $6.3M engagement, that 52% headline margin lands as about $757K of operating income.

The implication: Slalom needs roughly a 52% engagement margin to hit a 12% operating income target. So a "small" five-point discount isn't five points off profit -- it's a disproportionate hit to the only number that ultimately counts. That's why a margin concession should be the last lever you reach for, not the first.

Table stakes

Standard Contracting

Clients -- and sometimes we -- call these "creative." They're not. They're either how consulting has always been sold, or a straightforward lever (price, location, timing) dressed up as strategy.

T&M (Time & Materials)

Ready nowOfficial: Traditional

How consulting used to be sold, full stop. Pay for hours, regardless of outcome.

When to consider

Best reserved for genuinely open-ended, exploratory work where scope can't yet be pinned down. Treat it as a stepping stone toward a fixed-price or outcome-based model once scope firms up, not a resting state -- and never use T&M to carry outcome risk, which belongs in a priced fixed-fee structure.

Increasingly taboo with clients -- it reads as "pay for effort," not "pay for value," and procurement teams now push back on it by default.

Straight discounts

Ready now

Cutting the rate card to win the deal.

When to consider

Really only a last resort -- a low-strategic-value deal, under time pressure, where there's no room left to restructure. Work the lever ladder below first: a margin concession should be the last thing you reach for, not the first.

Not creative -- this is just saying yes to the client's ask. No structure, no shared risk, just a smaller number.

Allshore delivery

Ready now

Blending in lower-cost delivery locations to bring the average rate down.

When to consider

Best when the work itself splits cleanly into onshore/offshore-friendly pieces and the client is genuinely comfortable with a blended delivery model -- not as a reflex answer to any rate pressure. It's one of the seven pricing levers, and a legitimate one to pull before touching margin.

This is an efficient delivery decision, not a commercial one -- the risk profile between Slalom and the client hasn't moved at all.

Fixed price

Ready nowOfficial: Traditional

Bundling the scope into a single number.

When to consider

Best when scope is well defined and stable -- a discrete project or phase -- rather than an evolving, ambiguous programme. It's also the right container for any outcome-based premium, since outcome risk should never be carried on T&M.

A standard commercial construct now -- most enterprise buyers expect it as the baseline, not as evidence of a creative partner.

Fees at risk

Ready nowOfficial: Fees At Risk

Invoice a standard portion (e.g. 85%) and hold the remainder back, payable only if agreed performance conditions are met.

When to consider

Best on larger, milestone-heavy programmes where the client's governance already expects a performance holdback, or when you want to show a wary client that Slalom will put skin in the game. Worth knowing Slalom formally treats this as an alternative pricing structure -- but in enterprise MSAs it's now the entry ticket, not the differentiator.

Margin & commercial impact

Risks longer invoicing cycles while waiting for client sign-off, potential margin loss on missed SLAs, and unbilled time if assumptions aren't tightly defined. Upside is a fee uplift if you over-deliver -- and it can help sell AI-led delivery to a sceptical buyer.

More sophisticated than a discount, but it's now a standard clause in most enterprise MSAs -- procurement asks for it as a baseline.

Volume-based discounting

Ready now

Problem

Client asks for a cheaper hourly or day rate.

Standard fix

Offer a discounted rate in exchange for a volume commitment, softened with a three-month break clause. In practice, clients rarely exercise the break once the team beds in.

When to consider

Best when the client can commit to real, sustained volume -- a genuine multi-team programme. Slalom's standard framework tiers this by annual spend, and it stacks with medium/large project and procurement-introduction discounts, so check the framework before inventing a number.

Still just a volume discount -- the break clause makes it easier to sign, not more creative.
Genuine creativity

Creative Commercials

These change who carries the risk, tie fees to something real, or fund a discount from somewhere other than margin. This is where deals actually get won on structure, not price.

Effective discount engineering

Ready now

Problem

Procurement needs to see a bigger discount number to feel comfortable awarding -- even when the commercial reality is already tight.

Solution

Calculate the discount on an "effective" basis: fold in things like COLA adjustments you won't pass through across a year-end, or resource already invested in the account, to arrive at a larger headline discount figure.

When to consider

Best when procurement needs a bigger discount headline than the deal can genuinely absorb, and there are real costs to fold in. The test is evidential: every component must be a cost Slalom can actually point to, so the number stands up if procurement asks you to break it down line by line. If you can't evidence it, it isn't an effective discount \u2014 it's a story.

Margin & commercial impact

Doesn't touch true margin at all -- it's a presentation exercise, not a giveaway. But it only works once per deal: don't let the same COLA hold get reused as a "discount" again at renewal.

Example: GSK

Capacity subscription

Ops build requiredOfficial: Subscription

Problem

Procurement wants a lower unit cost and predictable spend, without re-approving a rigid SOW every time scope shifts.

Solution

Move from project billing to a fixed monthly retainer for access to a flexible pool of capability -- an "evergreen" backlog. It avoids the optics of a discount entirely, and creates stickier, easier-to-renew revenue for Slalom.

When to consider

Best for accounts with a steady, ongoing need for flexible capability rather than a single scoped project. Note that true subscription pricing is flagged in Slalom's official taxonomy as requiring significant effort to operationalise -- if you need something faster, a fixed-capacity POD gets you most of the way with far less friction.

Margin & commercial impact

Retainer revenue is stickier but often lower margin per hour than project work in isolation -- model the annualised account value, not the headline rate.

Rate-lock partnership pricing

Ready now

Problem

Procurement wants year-on-year cost certainty, or protection against inflationary rate rises across a multi-year deal.

Solution

Offer a two- to three-year rate lock with a small built-in escalator, well below true inflation -- framed and positioned as strategic partnership pricing, not as a discount line.

When to consider

Best on multi-year strategic accounts where the client values certainty more than the lowest possible starting number. Slalom's default position is an annual increase of the greater of CPI or 4%, so any lock below that is a real concession -- price it as one.

Margin & commercial impact

Exposes Slalom to real cost-inflation risk if the escalator is set too low -- model your own cost trajectory before locking a rate, not just the client's budget cycle.

Deferred payments / front-loaded discount

Ready now

Problem

Client asks for a discount, but the real issue is a timing problem -- cashflow pressure, a budget freeze, a year-end that's landed badly -- not a genuine view that the price is wrong.

Solution

Solve the actual problem instead of the stated one: defer payment terms, or front-load the "discount" into the first few invoices, while holding full contract value across the term.

When to consider

Best when a client's ask is really about timing, not value -- worth a candid conversation before assuming it's a straight price objection.

Margin & commercial impact

Contract value is unchanged -- this is a working-capital trade, not a margin concession. Just confirm Slalom's own cash position can absorb the deferral before offering it.

Fixed-capacity POD

Ready now

Problem

Client wants lower rates and is running a steady stream of change initiatives, each currently scoped, contracted and staffed as its own little project.

Solution

Replace project-by-project contracting with a fixed-capacity POD at a blended rate. The rate reduction is funded by discounting bench time, locking rates for twelve months, and sharing the contracting efficiency -- not by cutting margin. Onboarding and offboarding costs fall, institutional knowledge stays put, and both sides can plan further ahead.

When to consider

Best where a client has a continuous portfolio of change work rather than discrete projects, and where the churn of repeated scoping and contracting is itself a real cost. It's the pragmatic, available-today alternative to a full subscription model.

Margin & commercial impact

Proven to hold healthy margin while cutting the client's blended rate -- the discount comes from efficiency, not concession. Watch utilisation against committed capacity, since the POD only pays off if it stays genuinely full.

Example: Madrigal

The discretionary bonus

Ready now

Problem

Client's procurement has a fixed discount % or price cap in mind -- or the business needs to land under a threshold to avoid triggering a full procurement process.

Solution

Meet their rate, but agree a discretionary 0-3% "bonus," payable entirely at the client's discretion each quarter. In practice it's paid roughly nine times out of ten -- the client gets the number they needed to close, and Slalom protects the margin that would otherwise have been set away for good.

When to consider

Best when procurement needs to hit a specific discount % or price cap -- or the business needs to land under an approval threshold -- and the relationship is strong enough that a discretionary payment is genuinely likely to be honoured.

Margin & commercial impact

Protects margin far better than a straight discount -- paid roughly nine times out of ten in practice -- but forecast it as at-risk revenue, not committed revenue, until it's actually paid.

Example: AbbVie

Master SOW & drawdown

Ready now

Problem

Every engagement gets scoped, re-negotiated and re-approved from scratch. Contracting takes months, rates get re-litigated each time, and value leaks at every step before anyone does any work.

Solution

Put a single pre-agreed Master SOW framework in place and draw down against it. One rate negotiation up front instead of one per engagement, days to start work instead of weeks, and far fewer approval steps. Value gets captured through upfront MSOW discounting, tiered drawdown credits, reduced procurement and legal rework, or extra delivery capacity at the same spend.

When to consider

Best on accounts already spending at real scale where contracting friction is the actual bottleneck. A six-month pilot with an agreed value-capture mechanism is the natural way in before scaling it across the account.

Margin & commercial impact

Converts negotiation friction into delivery velocity -- the same spend buys more capability once contracting overhead is amortised across the portfolio. Agree the value-capture mechanism explicitly up front, or the efficiency simply becomes an unpriced client gain.

Asset co-investment

Ops build requiredOfficial: Royalty

Problem

Client wants a lower rate; Slalom wants to be more than a staff-aug line item on their books.

Solution

Co-fund a reusable accelerator or asset as part of the engagement in exchange for a lower blended rate -- while Slalom retains reuse or resale rights across other clients. The discount is funded by IP, not margin.

When to consider

Best when there's a genuine reusable asset worth building and a credible path to reuse or resell it elsewhere. Slalom is already investing to develop, test and maintain AI tools and accelerators, so pricing that investment into deals is how it gets recouped rather than quietly absorbed.

Margin & commercial impact

Margin is invested up front against a longer-term return. Track the payback across the whole portfolio of accounts it gets reused on, not this one deal in isolation. Anything touching IP ownership or resale needs legal and leadership involved early.

Satisfaction-based compensation

Leadership sign-offOfficial: Satisfaction Based

Problem

Client wants proof that Slalom will be held accountable for how the work actually lands, not just whether it was delivered.

Solution

Adjust a slice of Slalom's compensation up or down against the client's measured satisfaction, assessed through a formal survey or feedback mechanism agreed in advance.

When to consider

Best where there's a close, high-trust stakeholder relationship and you can make the questionnaire as objective as possible -- did we hit the timeline, did we meet 90% of SLAs -- rather than leaving it to sentiment. Ask early who signs it off if your main sponsor moves on.

Margin & commercial impact

Keep the at-risk portion small: the maximum possible loss should still leave the engagement above roughly 40-45% margin, and it should never create a loss position. It adds relatively little margin value on its own, so use it as a trust signal alongside a stronger construct rather than as the main event.

Land-and-expand insurance

Ready now

Problem

Client is openly nervous about Slalom pushing scope or upselling aggressively once we're in the door -- often after a bad experience with another vendor.

Solution

Reassure them structurally: build heavily discounted rates specifically into any Slalom-led cross-sell or up-sell, while holding full rate on the core scope and on any additional work the client comes to us and asks for. It removes Slalom's incentive to oversell, without discounting anything they actually value.

When to consider

Best early in a new relationship where trust is still being built, or after a client has been burned by scope creep from a previous vendor.

Margin & commercial impact

No impact on current scope margin at all -- it only discounts hypothetical future work that Slalom would otherwise have to fight hard to win anyway.

Discovery at risk, credited forward

Ready now

Problem

"Your discovery price is too high" -- or, "why would we pay you to learn how our business operates?"

Solution

Deliver discovery at risk -- free, or at Slalom's internal cost -- and issue it as a credit note against the full implementation phase that follows. It's an effective discount of up to 100% on phase one, funded entirely by the deal it unlocks.

When to consider

Best when the discovery or diagnostic phase is a genuine, unavoidable gate before a much larger implementation, and you're confident enough in the follow-on to treat the cost as an investment rather than a favour.

Margin & commercial impact

Full margin loss on discovery unless the implementation is signed -- track it as an at-risk investment on the account P&L, not a sunk cost, and agree a clear go/no-go point before starting. If the client can terminate early, make sure a termination fee protects the investment.

Gain share (outcome-linked)

Leadership sign-offOfficial: Gain Share

Problem

Client wants Slalom to have real skin in the game on business results -- not just on hitting delivery milestones.

Solution

Share in the financial gains the client actually realises -- cost reductions, productivity improvements, accelerated delivery -- typically at a pre-agreed multiple of the measurable improvement. Keep it distinct from fees at risk: that protects the client from Slalom underdelivering, whereas this rewards Slalom for the client overachieving.

When to consider

Strongest where a problem has genuinely defeated other vendors, where a hard deadline carries real financial consequences, or where a quantifiable cost or output improvement is available against a clear baseline. Outcomes should be objective and measured weekly or monthly for clean sign-off and invoicing.

Margin & commercial impact

Higher risk should mean higher margin -- price this at a premium, and always as a fixed fee rather than T&M. Expect slow benefit realisation, real setup effort in contracting and systems, and disagreements over the share split if the baseline isn't nailed down first.

Revenue share

Leadership sign-offOfficial: Revenue Share

Problem

The work will directly drive new client revenue, and the client would rather pay out of that upside than fund the whole build from budget they don't have.

Solution

Slalom takes a share of the incremental revenue generated as a direct result of the work. In practice this often means Slalom funds part of the project cost up front and earns it back at an agreed multiple over a set payback window -- with the multiple sliding down if Slalom is repaid early, and up if repayment is delayed.

When to consider

Best when success is tied to genuine revenue growth, the client's revenue projection is credible and shared, and both executive teams are aligned behind a single vision of success. Do the value quantification properly up front -- it's the whole basis of the deal.

Margin & commercial impact

Higher margin potential than traditional T&M or fixed fee, but slow to realise and demanding to set up in contracting and internal systems. Watch working capital across the payback period, and secure a single PO and a letter of intent for the sole-sourced SOW before starting.

The Glidepath

Leadership sign-off

Problem

Client is nervous about Slalom "landing and expanding," or staying artificially sticky -- and a straight volume discount or price-to-win line item feels too obvious to sign off.

Solution

Position the investment as AI-driven efficiency Slalom expects to realise over time -- e.g. ten resources priced as seven, on the basis that AI-accelerated ways of working close the gap. Go further with a gainshare: for every additional resource Slalom rolls off through automation, Slalom keeps 70% of the freed fees and the client keeps 30% (on fixed-price deals).

When to consider

Best on large, multi-year, managed-service-style engagements where AI or automation efficiency is real and measurable. Be honest about AI maturity: outcome-weighted structures like this only hold up once the solution is proven, the outcomes are controllable and the data is good. Early-stage AI work belongs on a fixed fee with conservative assumptions instead.

Margin & commercial impact

Margin only improves if the efficiency gains are real and tracked. Agree up front exactly how "resource rolled off" is measured, or this quietly becomes a discount wearing an AI badge.

Before You Concede

Trade, don't discount.

Price should be an output of the deal's shape, not an input you negotiate in isolation. Each of these seven levers runs in two directions: one end justifies a genuinely lower price, the other legitimately commands a premium. Margin is the eighth lever, and only after the rest are exhausted.

Pitfalls & things to avoid

Most of these constructs go wrong the same way: something meant to be occasional or conditional quietly becomes permanent and expected. A few common traps worth checking for before signing.

!

Rebates with no sunset clause

A discretionary bonus paid quarter after quarter stops feeling discretionary and starts feeling contractual. Clients budget for it, procurement cites it at the next renewal, and removing it reads as a price increase even though it was never guaranteed. Always attach a review point.

!

Volume discounts with no real exit

If the break clause is decorative rather than genuinely exercisable, Slalom ends up delivering at a discounted rate long after volumes have dropped. Track utilisation against the committed volume, and mean the break clause when you write it.

!

Discounting without an "effective" narrative

A straight percentage off the rate card sets a new, lower anchor for every future renewal and every future deal that references it. Procurement remembers the number, not the context behind it.

!

Credit notes without margin visibility

Free discovery or fees-at-risk work only makes sense if someone is actually tracking the cost against a clear payback point. Without that, it quietly becomes a write-off nobody signed up for.

!

Outcome risk priced on T&M

Outcome-driven models are inherently riskier and should carry a premium inside a fixed-fee structure. Carrying that risk on T&M means Slalom takes the downside without ever pricing in the upside.

!

Subjective success criteria

If the client says we missed it and we say we hit it, there's no mechanism to resolve it. Make satisfaction and outcome criteria as objective as possible -- did we meet the timeline, did we hit 90% of SLAs -- and agree who adjudicates before you sign.

!

Gainshare with no measurement agreement

If "efficiency gained" isn't defined and agreed with the client up front, these become disputed at exactly the moment they're supposed to start paying off. Set the baseline, and agree in advance whether and when it can be adjusted.

!

Land-and-expand insurance applied too broadly

If the discount accidentally covers work the client asked for -- not just Slalom-led upsell -- it erodes margin on core scope for no reason. Scope the clause tightly.

Before you sign: the legal & risk checklist

For any alternative pricing structure, involve Slalom Legal early and often. In practice it's rarely our legal team that blocks these -- it's the client's. Three questions decide whether a clever structure survives contact with a contract.

Measurement & baselining
  • Can we measure every output that triggers an invoice?
  • How long is the measurement period? More frequent is better.
  • Do we need a baselining or pilot period first?
  • Can the baseline be adjusted later, and who agrees that?
  • Are we monitoring for seasonality in the data?
Invoicing
  • Is there a client sign-off or acceptance period before we can invoice? Keep it as short as possible.
  • Is anything about that acceptance subjective?
  • Is there a single PO covering the compensation model?
  • How long before collection, and what does that do to working capital?
Termination
  • Do we need a no-termination-for-convenience period?
  • Is a termination fee required?
  • What deliverable payments or invested effort do we lose if the client exits early?
  • Are roles and responsibilities defined clearly enough on both sides to prove who caused what?

The test for whether it's actually creative

Two questions, and it needs to pass one of them. Has anyone's risk actually moved? Or, is the concession funded from somewhere other than margin — shared upside, recovered efficiency, reusable IP, working capital, or a discount headline built from costs you can evidence?

If neither is true, the client is simply paying less and Slalom is simply earning less. That's a discount, however it's dressed.
